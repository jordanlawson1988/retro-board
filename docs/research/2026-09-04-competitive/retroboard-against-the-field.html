Bottom line

RetroBoard is already the cheapest dedicated retro tool for a small team by a wide margin, and it has the in-meeting mechanics reviewers praise most: joining by link with no account, anonymous cards, hidden-until-reveal, secret voting, a synced timer. What it lacks is the second half of the category checklist. Everything that happens after the meeting (action follow-through, history, Jira, Slack), AI synthesis, and depth of templates and facilitation are all missing. Those are exactly the things people cite when they leave a tool, and in 2026 they show up in $10 tools and in some free tiers.

"Everything" is the wrong target. The evidence points at about ten capabilities that decide selection for small teams. Ship those and the pitch writes itself: every table-stakes feature, no per-seat math, participants never sign up, boards never expire, $4.99 a month.

The enterprise rows (SSO, SCIM, audit logs, SOC 2) and the long tail (public API, whiteboard canvas, planning poker, native apps) can wait until a customer asks with a purchase order.

Sequence matters. Nothing in the gap list is required to start charging, so the paywall ships first. Wave 1 of the build plan follows in the same month and turns "cheap" into "obvious."

Where RetroBoard stands today

Checked against the develop branch on September 4, not the May feature tracker. Reviewers' favorite in-meeting mechanics are all present. The gaps cluster around what happens between meetings.

Have

  • Join by link, no account, unlimited participants
  • Anonymous-card mode and secret voting
  • Cards hidden until the facilitator reveals
  • Dot voting with per-person limits, live vote sorting
  • Synced timer, board lock, card merge, emoji reactions
  • Four board views (grid, swimlane, list, timeline)
  • Action items with assignee, due date, status
  • Real-time presence, dark mode, mobile web
  • Personal dashboard, board roles, Markdown and CSV export
  • Boards never expire

Partial

  • Guided facilitation: the toggles exist, there is no one-click stage flow
  • History: boards persist per user, with no team view or trends
  • Async input: boards stay open, with no open window, reminders, or auto-reveal
  • Templates: four plus custom columns, and no way to save one
  • Share link: the join link doubles as the read-only link

Missing

  • AI summary, auto-grouping, suggested actions
  • Action-item carry-over across retros
  • Jira, Linear, GitHub, Azure DevOps push
  • Slack and Microsoft Teams
  • Icebreakers, check-ins, mood pulse, health check
  • Comments and mentions on cards, images on cards
  • PDF export, email summary
  • Google or Microsoft sign-in
  • Any language other than English
  • SSO, SCIM, audit logs, public API

The price gap is real, and it is not a moat

Monthly cost for an eight-person team on each tool's cheapest paid plan. Every dedicated tool with verified pricing lands between two and thirteen times RetroBoard's $4.99.

Two readings of this chart matter more than the gap itself.

The real competition below $10 is free. RetroTool's free plan already includes unlimited participants, secret voting, a timer, action items, and Markdown export. Mural's free plan includes voting, a timer, and private mode. Parabol gives two teams ten meetings a month for nothing, and Reetro meters out free AI summaries. What those free tiers withhold is history (7 to 30 days, or 12 months at RetroTool), facilitation depth, and integrations. That is the list RetroBoard has to win on, because price alone does not beat free.

The benchmark above $10 is TeamRetro, not the famous names. At $20.83 it ships AI grouping and summaries, an AI assistant, Jira two-way sync, Slack and Teams publishing, health checks, eight UI languages, SOC 2, and it released something nearly every month of 2025 and 2026. EasyRetro ships small quality-of-life updates; Retrium and GoRetro show no product movement at all since 2023. The category is splitting into fast movers and stalled tools, and TeamRetro is the one to watch.

Why teams leave, in order of how often it came up: no history, missing or broken integrations, price too high for the use case, action items tracked by hand outside the tool, clunky grouping, a free plan being removed, and limits on customizing columns.

The AI finding deserves a plain statement. Vendors are shipping AI as fast as they can, and it now appears in free tiers, so it has to be on the checklist. But almost nobody says they picked a tool because of it. One good summary plus suggested groups and suggested actions covers the checklist. Do not bet the product on an AI facilitator.

What "no-brainer" actually requires

A team choosing a retro tool in late 2026 is comparing against a free whiteboard, a free tier that expires, or TeamRetro at $21. RetroBoard becomes the obvious answer when a facilitator can say all of the following without a caveat:

Participants never sign up. Anonymous cards and secret voting. AI summary and suggested groups. Actions go to Jira, and open ones roll into the next retro. Summary posts to Slack. 25 templates or build your own. PDF for the manager. History never expires. One flat $4.99 a month, however many people show up.

That sentence needs ten capabilities, not sixty. Here they are, in the order the evidence supports:

  1. AI retro summary, suggested groupings, suggested action items
  2. Open-action carry-over into the next retro, plus an action tracker on the dashboard
  3. Push action items to Jira, then Linear and GitHub through the same code path
  4. Share summary and actions to Slack and Microsoft Teams
  5. A curated library of 25 to 30 templates, and save-as-template
  6. PDF export
  7. Guided facilitation: one-click Write, Group, Vote, Discuss, Actions
  8. Icebreaker question bank and a one-question mood pulse, trended over time
  9. Team spaces with team history and recurring themes
  10. Async mode: an open window, reminders, auto-reveal at meeting time

Three conversion items cost almost nothing and belong in the same window: Google and Microsoft sign-in (creators now have to sign up before their first board), comparison pages and a trust page (every competitor runs "X alternative" pages; you have none), and a visible support promise. Support responsiveness is a stated reason people love small tools, and a one-person company can win it outright.

Localization is the one bet the field does not point to but your own database does. Four of your six real signups run retros in Spanish, German, or Russian. Only TeamRetro ships more than one language. Spanish and Portuguese first would be a differentiated move at low cost once the strings are extracted.

Do not build these yet

  • SSO, SAML, SCIM, audit logs, SOC 2, data residency. Enterprise procurement gates, not small-team selection drivers. Revisit when a real buyer asks.
  • A public API, webhooks, Zapier. Rare even among the smaller tools.
  • A whiteboard canvas, planning poker, or standups. That is Spreo's and Kollabe's bundle fight, not yours.
  • Native mobile apps. Nobody has them; the mobile web overhaul is enough.
  • An AI that runs the meeting. Async Retro is pre-launch on that pitch, and no reviewer asked for it.

Next steps

The go-paid checklist from September 3 is unchanged. The price is now confirmed at $4.99 flat, unlimited boards and members. What this analysis adds is the build order that follows the flip.

  1. Confirm the two open paywall decisions. Free tier: one active board (built) or one lifetime board (your August ask, recommended, half a day). Existing owners: comp all four into the allowlist or not (recommended not).
  2. Start Paddle today. Sandbox is instant. Submit the live account for verification the same day; it is the long pole, and the terms, privacy, and refund pages it needs are already live.
  3. Resume the billing branch. Rebase onto develop, then Tasks 5 through 13: Paddle client and webhook, entitlement endpoint, upgrade modal, pricing section, allowlist admin, email verification, sandbox end-to-end, banner, ship. About three developer days. Update the $3.99 copy in the terms and refund pages.
  4. Brainstorm and plan Wave 1 item one in parallel. The AI summary does not depend on billing, so its design and plan can run while Paddle verification is pending. Same for the Jira push.
  5. Write the pricing and comparison copy from the eight-person math. "$4.99 flat versus $21 to $64" and "participants never sign up" are the two lines every competitor's reviews say matter. Add a support promise and a trust page.
  6. Flip when Paddle approves. Seed the allowlist, merge to main, smoke on prod, buy Pro with a real card, refund yourself. Then ship Wave 1 in the same month.

Confidence and sources

High confidence: pricing and features for TeamRetro, Neatro, Parabol, GoRetro, Kollabe, RetroTool, Mural, Miro, and FigJam, all read from primary pricing pages on September 4. The buyer-sentiment findings anchored on Capterra reviews and vendor changelogs (selection drivers, churn drivers, pricing psychology, the 2025 to 2026 launch list).

Medium confidence: EasyRetro and Retrium, whose pricing pages block automated reads, so figures come from dated secondary sources. Reetro, where a dated source says $10 a month and an undated aggregator says $39. Retrium's AI features, which appear only in third-party listings and not on its own site.

Low confidence: Spreo's dollar pricing (the page renders no numbers), Retros.work (site timed out), RetroBox pricing (inside Slack only), Async Retro (pre-launch). Neatro's SOC 2 status is contradicted between two sources. Community sentiment is under-sampled: Reddit, G2's pros-and-cons pages, and TrustRadius refused automated reads, so the "what people recommend" picture leans on dev.to posts and vendor comparison pages.

Not verified for most tools: native mobile apps, dark mode, accessibility statements, keyboard shortcuts, social login. Treat "none verified" in those rows as unknown, not absent.

Method: four parallel research passes (six leading dedicated tools; twelve smaller, chat-native, and new tools; whiteboards and suites; buyer sentiment and changelogs), about 140 page reads, a fixed 60-row capability taxonomy so results merge, then synthesis against the RetroBoard codebase. The full per-tool profiles, matrices, contradictions, and every source URL are in the four research files saved with this session.
